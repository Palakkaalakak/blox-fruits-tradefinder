# Economy, and the Great Council

## What makes a state rich

**Industry and trade — amplified enormously by colonies and vassals.**

Two engines, and a multiplier.

**Industry.** Whoever can *make* things — aircraft, medicines, weapons, machines — dominates whoever must buy them. This is the Dark Age lesson made permanent: knowledge was never scarce, capacity was. The powers that recovered manufacture first have stayed ahead ever since, because each advance made the incumbents richer rather than displacing them.

**Trade.** Goods must move, and moving them is where fortunes are made and taxed. This puts the **Almani Corridor** at the centre of the world economy: the single strait where Lidia and Réselia touch, through which the traffic between them must pass. Cevine holds both shores. Cevine taxes the world.

**The multiplier: colonies and vassals.** Neither engine scales without them. Colonies on the third continent supply raw materials below market cost and consume finished goods above it — the classic closed circuit. Vassals do domestically what colonies do abroad: supply men, grain, ore and revenue upward while buying manufactures downward.

## What this implies

- **Wealth is a pyramid, and it is visible.** A great power is one with industry *and* a trade position *and* dependencies feeding both. Cevine has all three, which is why it is paramount.
- **The Rediscovery still pays.** Colonial extraction is not history; it is current income. That is why nobody stops it, and why the free states of the third continent are resented — every one of them is a leak in the circuit.
- **Vassalage is economic, not sentimental.** Karois-Marain's hundred-plus direct vassals are not decoration; they are the revenue base. And Parvaan's secession was not a matter of pride. It was a hole in the pyramid, and worse, a *demonstration* — which is motive enough for Ouspré.
- **A state can be rich without being sovereign, and sovereign without being rich.** Edward's parents built a sovereign duchy famous for marble — a commodity state. Small, independent, and utterly dependent on someone else's industry and someone else's shipping.
- **Ramwin teaches this.** Mathematics as the ratios of coercion and loyalty, economics as the thing that keeps a realm afloat. The curriculum is downstream of the economy.

### Open
- Where is heavy industry concentrated? Whoever holds it holds the world
- Who controls shipping and insurance? In a trade economy that is quieter power than a fleet
- Is there a currency, or many? A hundred-odd realms suggests many, and therefore banking
- Does salvage from the drowned world still yield anything of value?

## The Great Council

**It convened once. It has never met again.**

All the rulers of the world, of every rank, gathered after the murder of Queen Renilda and voted — almost unanimously, for the first time in history — to found the Academy. Then it dissolved.

## Why this is the strongest choice

The Academy is the **only surviving product of a moment of unity the world can no longer reproduce.** Not an institution, not the start of an order. A single miracle, already over.

That gives you:

- **The children are the entire inheritance.** Sixty heirs in a glass building are not one initiative among several. They are the whole of what the world managed. If they fail, nothing else was built.
- **The adults have already given up.** They did the impossible thing once and went home. Everything they said in that room about peace and unity was true when they said it and has not been acted on since. Mrs Herseworth's "your parents could not create a world of harmony, but they hope that you will" is the confession of a generation that tried exactly once.
- **The Academy has no protector.** With no standing council, there is no body to defend the school when a realm decides it dislikes what its heir is learning, or when the peace it represents becomes inconvenient. It exists on inertia and goodwill. Both are finite.
- **Unanimity among a hundred-odd realms was seismic.** Getting fifteen rulers to agree is a good afternoon; getting a hundred and fifty is a miracle. Which measures precisely how enormous Renilda's murder was — and raises the question of who benefited from a world that shocked.

> Structurally: the founding is the world's *reditus magni*. A Great died, and instead of furor the world chose, once, to build something. Then it went back to being itself. Whether that single act was enough is the question the series exists to answer.

- Can it be convened again, and by whom?
