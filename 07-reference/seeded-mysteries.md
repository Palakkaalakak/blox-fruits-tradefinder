# Seeded mysteries — for readers, not for characters

*In-world scholarship is one thing. This is the other: material planted so that **readers** notice, argue, and theorise between books.*

## The rules

There is a real craft to this, and most of it is restraint.

1. **The clue must be noticeable and unexplained — never both explained and noticed.** A detail a character remarks on and then interprets is closed. A detail a character mentions and moves past is open. Almost all your seeding should be the second kind.
2. **It must be answerable.** Readers can tell the difference between a mystery with a solution and a mystery with a mood. Every seed below has an answer already decided, even where the answer is a long way off.
3. **It must be re-readable.** The test: on a second read, does the passage change meaning? If yes, it works. If it merely becomes conspicuous, it doesn't.
4. **Never confirm in the same book you plant.** Minimum one volume of silence.
5. **Bury it in ordinary business.** A clue delivered during a chase, an argument or a joke is a clue readers trust. A clue delivered during a lull reads as an announcement.
6. **Some seeds must be red herrings, and they must be fair.** They should have honest, mundane explanations that arrive later — not lies, just things that looked like more than they were. Without these, readers learn that every odd detail is load-bearing, and the real ones stop being work to find.

## The tiers

**Tier 1 — the reader can solve it with care.** Rewards attention within a book or two. This is where most seeding should sit.

**Tier 2 — the reader can theorise but not confirm.** Enough evidence to argue, never enough to close. This is what fandoms live on.

**Tier 3 — the reader cannot know yet, but on reread the groundwork is undeniable.** Reserved for the Elsring.

## The seeds

### The Elsring (Tier 3 — total silence)
Nobody in the world suspects anything, so no character can wonder aloud. Everything must be environmental and unremarked:

- A tremor in a region with no history of them, mentioned once, blamed on nothing
- A shaft, adit or sealed stair in an odd place — noted as a curiosity of an old estate, never entered
- A word in no known language, in a place-name or a folk song, that fits neither the Romance nor the Anglo register
- A map or chart that is more accurate than its supposed date allows
- Something structurally impossible in an old mine: too straight, too deep, too well made

None of these is ever discussed. A character sees it and thinks about something else. **That is the whole technique.**

### The lost continent (Tier 2 — argued in-world *and* out)
The strongest seed you have, because the in-world argument gives readers a legitimate frame to argue inside. Feed both sides honestly:

- Soundings that put a plateau where deep ocean should be
- A dredged object that shouldn't exist, dismissed by someone credible
- The land arithmetic not balancing — mentioned by a scholar as a known problem, unsolved
- A rival explanation that is *good*. It must not be obviously wrong

### The pole shift (Tier 1 — solvable, and quietly chilling)
- A pre-Doom star chart matching nothing overhead. Shown once, unremarked
- Two navigational instruments that disagree, treated as routine
- An old text describing a place at a latitude nothing occupies now — cited by a character for a completely different reason

### Queen Renilda's murder (Tier 2 — the engine)
Her death produced the only unanimous act in the world's history. Someone benefited from a world that shocked.

- Who was *not* at the Great Council
- Who voted against, given the text says "almost unanimously"
- Who proposed the school — currently unnamed, and the most consequential person of the age
- The timing: how fast the Council convened after the murder. Too fast is a clue

### The Doom's ending (Tier 2 — deliberately unanswerable in-world)
Since nobody agrees whether it's over, readers get to hold a position the characters can't settle. Feed it: a port that silted within living memory, an insurer who won't write certain coasts, a charted shoal two charts disagree about.

### Red herrings (necessary)
At least three details that feel enormous and turn out to be ordinary — a family's odd heirloom, an inexplicable absence, a coincidence of names. Resolve each mundanely, on the page, a book or two later. This is what keeps the real seeds honest work.

## What NOT to seed

- **Nothing that requires an out-of-world reference to solve.** Everything must be gettable from the text.
- **No clue whose only function is to be a clue.** Every planted detail must also serve the scene it's in — as character, texture, or plot. If it exists solely to be noticed, it announces itself.
- **No unanswerable ambiguity dressed as mystery.** If you don't know the answer, it isn't a seed, it's a hole. Readers can tell, eventually, and it's the fastest way to lose them.
